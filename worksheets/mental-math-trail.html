Grade 6 · Number Skills

Follow the trail to faster mental math

No calculator needed. Four simple stops teach you the tricks real mathematicians use to add, subtract and estimate in their heads.

1
Stop 1 · Addition trick

Number bonds to 10

Look for two digits that add up to 10 — they're a "bond." Split your numbers apart so the bond appears, then the rest is easy.

52 + 68 = 120

The units are 2 and 8 — they bond to make 10. So really we're solving 50 + 60 + 10.

The same idea works for subtraction — check for matching units instead.

146 − 36 = 110

Both numbers end in 6, so 6 − 6 = 0. That leaves the easy part: 140 − 30 = 110.

Try it: Before adding or subtracting, always ask — "do the units make a bond, or match exactly?"
2
Stop 2 · Rounding trick

Borrowed numbers

Some numbers are almost a round number. Round them, do the easy sum, then pay back what you borrowed.

79 + 32 = 111

79 is nearly 80. Borrow 1 to make it 80: 80 + 32 = 112. Now pay the 1 back: 112 − 1 = 111.

118 − 44 = 74

118 is nearly 120. Round up: 120 − 44 = 76. You added 2 to get there, so give it back: 76 − 2 = 74.

Golden rule: Whatever you add to make rounding easy, you must remove from the final answer — and vice versa.
3
Stop 3 · Negative numbers

Subtracting a negative

Watch what happens to 10 − ? as the number we subtract keeps shrinking, all the way past zero:

10 − 4 = 6
10 − 3 = 7
10 − 2 = 8
10 − 1 = 9
10 − 0 = 10
10 − (−1) = 11
10 − (−2) = 12
10 − (−3) = 13

Every step, the answer keeps climbing by 1 — even after we cross into negative numbers. That tells us something important:

10 − (−3) = 10 + 3 = 13

Subtracting a negative number works exactly like adding its positive twin. Two minus signs turn into a plus.

Remember: "Minus a minus" always makes the answer bigger, never smaller.
4
Stop 4 · Sanity check

Estimating with rounding

Before working out a big calculation, round every number to the nearest 10, 100, or 1000 first. This gives you a rough answer to check against later.

286 + 407 ≈ 700

286 rounds to 300. 407 rounds to 400. 300 + 400 = 700 — a quick, close estimate.

Estimating also helps you catch mistakes. A classmate calculates this in their head:

1121 + 788 − 210 − (−592) = 1291 ?

Rounding each number: 1000 + 800 − 200 − (−600) = 2200. Since 1291 is barely half of the estimate, the classmate's answer must be wrong — time to redo the working.

Habit to build: Estimate first, calculate second, then compare. If they're miles apart, check your work.
